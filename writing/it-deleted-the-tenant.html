It Did Not Protect the Tail. It Deleted the Tenant.

Scope. This is milestone M5-b of a personal project, a Kubernetes-native GPU control plane. The numbers come from a run on a real GPU — one A10G — on 2026-09-03 and an engine-level microtest on 2026-09-04. The raw evidence for both is in the repository. The project is in progress, and this article covers only what is settled as of 2026-09-05. One run has been pre-registered and not yet bought; its result changes the last section.

What was being measured: when a premium tenant shares one GPU with a long-context noisy neighbour, does the gateway's KV-cache-aware admission guard protect the premium tail?

The answer was no. The more valuable finding was why four paid runs could not see that.

The arm that makes it falsifiable#

Four arms, replaying one recorded open-loop trace under four conditions.

The third row is what makes the experiment falsifiable. Without static-cap, "the guard lowered p99" is indistinguishable from "the guard shed load" — and shedding load lowers p99 under any policy whatsoever.

The checks were registered in code before the run, so that the bar could not be moved after seeing the result.

  • absolute protection: kv-aware p99 at most 1.25x R1's
  • incremental value: kv-aware p99 at most 0.90x static-cap's, with the bootstrap CI's upper bound below 1.0
  • admission match: the two contended arms' admitted-work fractions within a frozen tolerance

A separate rule disqualifies a run before any check is read: if a compared arm completed no premium requests, has a censored tail (over 1% premium timeouts), or has too few completions. That last bound is derived rather than chosen — the percentile is nearest-rank, and ceil(0.99n)-1 clamps to n-1 for every n below 100. Under it the reported p99 is simply the slowest request.

The report printed FAIL for all three pre-registered checks. Absolute protection came in at 83.7x against a bar of 1.25x. The claim this milestone was built to make was withdrawn.

But two of the three are void rather than failed. Incremental value and admission match are both computed against static-cap as the control — and that arm was not a control. Only absolute protection compared what it was meant to compare, and it failed by a factor of 67.

Why it was not a control is the rest of this article.

Three sentences that cannot all be true#

Read static-cap as three statements:

  1. It rejected nothing (429 = 0).
  2. Yet it completed half as much as off.
  3. And its tail matches the isolation baseline — 81.2ms against 82.2ms.

A control was applied, it rejected nothing, and the outcome equals isolation. Those three cannot all be true at once.

The completion count gives it away. 1,840 is exactly the number of premium requests in this run. And off's 200 responses split into 1,840 premium plus 1,788 noisy. So static-cap rejected all 1,788 of them, without exception.

The admitted-work fraction of 0.0% is that statement in one number. static-cap matched isolation's latency not because it made the engine efficient, but because it deleted the competing tenant. It did not protect the tail; it removed what was producing one.

And why it deleted all of it was not policy but a configuration error, of two parts. First, the runner passed the trace's request rate (1.568/s) to a flag measured in tokens per second. Second, it never passed the burst flag at all, so the burst stayed at the gateway's 8,192 default while every noisy prompt in the trace estimates at about 10,000 tokens. A request larger than the burst can never fit any bucket, so the gateway refused all of them with 413, in all four repetitions.

So static-cap became a second isolation arm rather than a control. That is why the ratio against kv-aware (84.760) came out nearly equal to the ratio against isolation (83.747) — the two were measuring the same thing.

Why the report could not see it#

Two things stacked.

First, the gateway splits rejection into two kinds. 429 means "come back later"; 413 means "send something smaller". The distinction was deliberate — answering a request the bucket can never hold with a 429 makes the client retry an arithmetically impossible request forever. As a design decision it is right.

But the report counted only 429 as shedding. All 1,788 of static-cap's rejections were 413, so the row read "0 requests shed". A correct distinction dropped one whole side out of the accounting.

Second, every verdict the report produced was a ratio. Absolute protection and incremental value are ratios of tail latency; admission match is a ratio of admitted work. But even admission match only asks how close the two arms are to each other — it never names the fact that one of them is zero. Look only at ratios and "went faster while doing less" reads identically to "went faster while doing the same". A vanished denominator is not visible from a ratio.

Between them, this stayed invisible for four paid runs.

What made it visible was not a new run. It was re-scoring evidence already bought — attaching throughput and per-tenant share to the same 2026-09-03 raw data.

The lesson here is not about KV caches. A measurement that watches only the thing being protected cannot show you what was removed to protect it.

Forty cents answered what three paid runs could not#

Once the guard has failed, the next question is not "how do I fix the guard" but "is this the layer to fix?" The gateway sits outside the engine and cannot see how the engine packs requests into batches.

So the next test removed the gateway entirely and changed only engine settings. One long request and one short one, ten repetitions per cell, on a single instance rather than a cluster, in a public subnet where inbound transfer is free. It cost about $0.40.

Those two numbers need care side by side — 83.7x is a p99 over an arrival process with many concurrent prefills; 1.57x is a median over ten repetitions of two requests. They are not the same axis, so "83.7 dropped to 1.57" is the wrong reading. What can be said is this: the interference the gateway did nothing about comes out small on a far simpler load once only the engine settings change. It was a different layer. Put a control plane on top of a misconfigured engine and what you measure is the misconfiguration, not the control plane.

The table has to be read precisely, though. This is not the effect of one budget; it is a budget × policy interaction. Read the priority column alone and larger budgets get worse; the fcfs column moves the other way. They meet at 8,192. And only one of the six cells clears the bar, with the pattern still unexplained.

This microtest had its own pre-registered readings, and none of them held. The reading table could not express an interaction between two factors. Pre-registration stops a result being fitted after the fact; it does not stop the registered reading table from failing to contain the result.

The cost, written down: three paid sessions, roughly ten dollars of GPU and cluster time, and the first two produced no valid measurement. Everything after that — the mechanism, the re-scoring, two of my own explanations being falsified — came out of evidence already bought. And the experiment that located the layer cost forty cents, because the question had been narrowed to one that two requests could answer.

The run not yet bought#

That is everything settled as of 2026-09-05. The remaining question is pre-registered and has not been bought:

Is there a configuration that protects the premium tail without deleting the contending tenant's work, and what does it cost the tenant it protects?

It has that shape because of the table above. "Can the tail be protected" was already answered by two arms, one of which answered it by discarding all contending work. What is left is the price of protection.

The pre-registration is written before the run is bought, because a bar set after seeing the result can make any result a success. This project has already paid for that lesson once — a published result was withdrawn, and the withdrawal came after the numbers were in hand, which is the most expensive moment to discover you were measuring the wrong thing.

What this does not claim#

  • One comparison arm was void. static-cap admitted nothing because of a configuration error, making it a second isolation arm rather than a control. So two of the three pre-registered checks — incremental value and admission match — are void, not failed. The one that failed validly is absolute protection.
  • The microtest is not the effect of a budget alone. It is a budget × scheduling policy interaction, one of six cells clears the bar, and none of the pre-registered readings held.
  • Not a general claim that admission control cannot protect a tail. What was falsified is one signal — reactive KV occupancy, a 0.85 engage threshold, a 30-second release — on one workload, one model and one card.
  • Not a claim that engine settings fix this. The microtest is a median over ten repetitions of one long request and one short one. The four-arm run is an arrival process with many concurrent prefills, reported at p99. They are not the same axis.
  • Not a comparison across milestones on different silicon. Comparisons stay within one card class, model and dtype; a contract test enforces the last two.
  • It says nothing about cold starts. Every arm serves a warm engine.
  • The rest of this project still runs on a fake device plugin. These numbers come from real-GPU runs; the repository's other run records — the queue-policy lab, training admission, the failure evidence trail — do not, and the repository marks that per row.
  • It is in progress. When the pre-registered run above completes, the last section changes.

What is left is one line. A metric that measures protection, without also counting what was deleted, will read the deletion as a success. And finding that out cost no new money — it took re-reading evidence already bought along a different axis.
