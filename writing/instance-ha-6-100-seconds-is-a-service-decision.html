Instance HA ⑥: Whether 100 Seconds Is Enough Is a Service Decision
I started to write the recovery time as a single number and stopped. It only became a number a team could agree on after it was split into three stages and paired with a verdict per service type.

By part five the decision, the procedure and the foundation were all settled. One question was left. How long does recovery take under this design?

The design document says about 100 seconds. This post is about where that number came from and why it should not be handed over as it stands.

At first I tried to write it as a single number. Once it was written down, there was nothing the person receiving it could do with it. Nothing inside the number told them whether it was enough.

Who this is for#

  • Anyone who has to agree a recovery time target with their team
  • Anyone holding an RTO figure who cannot say whether it is good enough
  • Anyone asked to shorten detection who needs to weigh what that costs
  • Anyone who read from part one and wants to know where the design ended up

At first I tried to write it as one number#

A single number cannot do two things. It cannot point at where to cut when someone asks for a shorter time, and it cannot answer whether the time is acceptable. Both answers live in the structure behind the number, not in the number.

So I split it two ways. Along the time axis I broke it into stages, and along the people axis I broke it out by service type. The first says where to cut. The second says whether cutting is needed at all.

Splitting the estimate into three stages#

Recovery passes through three stages: reaching a verdict that the host is dead, cutting power and confirming it, and bringing the machine up elsewhere.

The longest stretch is not detection but the restart, and it also has the widest spread. Halving detection moves the total by only about fifteen percent. Finding where to cut requires splitting the stages first.

The same 100 seconds differs by service#

Splitting the time still does not answer whether it is enough. That answer does not belong to the design. It belongs to the service.

Verdict per service type.
Service typeVerdictReasoning
Stateless API serviceAcceptableRetries absorb it and no state is left behind
Database backed serviceRiskyData consistency across the downtime needs separate review
NFV and realtime serviceNot possibleNeeds an architecture in the 10 to 20 second range

One estimate, and the verdict splits three ways. NFV is not a figure this design can reach by tuning; it is a place where the architecture has to change. That is not a call an engineer makes alone.

This is why the design document records it as an RTO target requiring team agreement. The number was raised as something to agree on, not set as a goal already decided.

It can be shortened, but not for free#

There is a way to shorten it. The detection stage is governed by the external probe's TTL, so cutting the retry count takes 30 seconds down to 15.

# Current design: six missed probes at five second intervals means dead
probe_interval=5s
probe_retry=6      # 30s TTL

# Shortened option: three retries brings the TTL to fifteen seconds
probe_retry=3      # 15s TTL

# The cost: more room to read a momentary delay as a death

But as part two showed, that same TTL is what separates a momentary delay from a real stop. The shorter it is, the more room there is to read a delay as a death. What shrinks is the recovery time; what grows is the chance of a wrong call.

So the shortened option was written down together with its cost. Saying only that it can be shortened leaves the reader unable to decide.

How far the design document travelled#

What this series comes from is a design document. Another engineer picked up the implementation and I was not part of it. So saying whether this design survived means pointing at someone else's document rather than at my own words.

A follow up review written around the same time cites this design document. Its conclusion was that recovery must not run while the observation point itself is broken, which is the same content as the hold logic and the phase split in the design.

These are the improvements the design document claims. Which of them actually showed up in operation is outside what I can verify.

Limits: what this post does not claim#

  • ⚠️ The figure of about 100 to 160 seconds is an estimate, not a measurement. It adds up known durations per stage, and the design document itself marks it as needing team agreement.
  • I never hit this figure. Estimating it at design time and proposing the per service criteria is where my part ends.
  • The three service types are a way to make the call easier, not a claim that every service falls into one of them.
  • That a follow up document cites the design is confirmed in writing. The order the two documents were written in, and their authorship history, I could not confirm, so I claim no more than that.
  • This came from internal material, so product names, customers, addresses, accounts, hostnames, issue numbers and author names are left out.

Wrapping up#

One thing runs through all six parts. When the grounds for a decision are piled onto a single number or a single signal, there is no way out when that one thing is wrong. Part one was trusting one observation point and calling a live node dead. The five parts after it were all about dividing the grounds up.

Signals were divided by what each can testify to, failure detection from execution readiness, the command from the confirmation, consensus from fencing, and finally time itself into stages and into services.
