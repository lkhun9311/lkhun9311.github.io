Instance HA ①: We Judged a Live Node Dead
One switch died, the four networks ended up in different states, and a controller read its own isolation as the compute node's death. First part of a series on the Instance HA failure-response design.

Instance HA in this product evacuates the VMs off a compute host that has died and brings them up on a host that is still alive. Automatic recovery. The moment it turns dangerous is when a host that did not die is judged dead. The system then moves VMs that were running perfectly well, and with bad luck it corrupts their data.

That is what happened in March 2026. An L2/L3 switch died, but what it cut was not the compute nodes: it was the controllers talking to each other. In that state a leader controller judged a live compute node failed and fired Nova Evacuate at scale. I did not investigate the incident myself. I received the established facts and designed the judgement system meant to stop it recurring.

This is the first part of the series, and part one stops at the cause. How four network strands ended up in different states, why a controller read its own isolation as the target's death, and how far a wrongly fired recovery could have gone. Grading the signals and splitting the phases come later.

Who this is for#

  • Anyone designing or running a system that recovers dead nodes automatically
  • Anyone who has watched automation make a network partition worse
  • Anyone who has to decide which signal gets to say "the node is dead"
  • Anyone wondering why split-brain has to come before fast recovery

The day recovery became the incident#

The incident fits in one line. During an L2/L3 switch failure a leader controller judged a live compute node failed and fired unnecessary Instance HA at scale. Healthy VMs moved to other hosts at the same time, and in the worst case a replica would attach to the same volume while the original was still alive, corrupting the filesystem.

What matters here is not that recovery was late. It is that a recovery which should never have run did run. Failures in that direction are far harder to undo.

Four strands hung off one switch#

The crux is this. When a switch dies, each network ends up in a different state. One strand being cut does not mean all of them are. This farm had four strands, and when the failure came their fates diverged.

Once controller-to-controller traffic is cut the cluster quorum collapses and some controllers start judging on their own. Yet MGMT was up, so that compute node's agent heartbeat was arriving normally. The node that had been declared dead was reporting its own health, steadily.

Unreachable and dead were not told apart#

"I cannot reach that node" can mean two things. It can mean the node died, or it can mean the path between us is cut. They are completely different situations that look identical from outside.

The root of the incident was mistaking the observer's isolation for the target's failure. If the isolated side cannot feed its own isolation into the judgement, no amount of observational accuracy changes the conclusion. The question was never what to observe more sharply. It was whose word to believe.

A recovery that should not have run breaks the volume#

What makes the misjudgement expensive comes next. If the original VM is still alive and writing to its disk while a replica attaches to the same volume on another host, both sides write to the same place. What breaks is not one VM. It is the whole volume.

This is why the design ends up defaulting to "stop when you cannot be sure" rather than "recover fast". The asymmetry of the cost set the judgement.

What to recover was already decided in two layers#

One thing was already in the product when the design started: the axis that picks recovery targets is two layers, the host control attribute and the Instance metadata. I had walked all three values in September 2024 while verifying the feature.

If the host attribute is HA_Enabled, only instances carrying that marker in metadata become targets. If it is HA_Disabled, everything without the marker becomes a target. If it is NONE, everything becomes a target regardless of metadata. Opt-in, opt-out and all, selectable per host.

At the time I checked the excluded list as carefully as the target list. In a recovery feature the dangerous half is not what fails to be recovered but what gets recovered and should not. When the 2026 design later put seven of eight scenarios on hold, the premise that a decision point for "recover or not" already existed in the product came from here.

Limits: what this post does not claim#

  • ⚠️ What this series produced is a design document. Implementation was picked up by another engineer, and how far it reached the shipped product is outside this post.
  • I did not investigate the incident. I received the established facts and designed the judgement system that followed. What misjudged was the existing recovery logic.
  • The 2024 work was feature verification rather than design or implementation, and its evidence is screen records rather than automated tests.
  • This is internal material. Product names, customers, server addresses, hostnames and accounts are not used, and the network layout is generalised only as far as the judgement structure needs.

Closing#

One thing is worth carrying out of this part. Automatic recovery turns dangerous not when recovery is slow but when the observer judges the target without knowing its own state. A single switch died, four strands met different fates, and the surviving strand was saying the node was alive while the side holding a cut strand made the call.

The next part is what we changed because of it: instead of sharpening detection, deciding what each signal is entitled to testify to. Continue to part two.
