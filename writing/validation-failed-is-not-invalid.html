Logging Out When Validation Fails Evicts Only the Busiest Users
The browser drives the polling, so for anyone who stepped away nothing validates in the first place.

I build the backend for an internal cloud admin console. At one site the login is owned by an upstream identity portal, and the console asks that portal, on a timer, whether the session is still valid.

At that site users were reportedly thrown back to the portal mid-task, with no message at all. It never reproduced in our own environment.

The logs said the trigger was not a verdict of ours. While the portal was unstable it pushed a session-expired notice down its stream, and we were relaying it as-is. The first prescription anyone offered for this was “if we cannot validate, log the user out to be safe”. This is about why that was rejected and what went in instead.

Who this is for#

  • You build a screen that asks an external identity server whether a session still holds
  • “If it cannot be confirmed, deny” is your default
  • You have tried to protect a session with a periodic check the browser runs

Users were thrown out in silence#

The symptom is one line. The screen someone was working on disappears and the portal's front page is there instead. No warning, no reason. All the user can see is that what they were doing is gone.

Site logs showed that at the same moment two of the portal's validation endpoints were returning 500 and refusing connections. Calls were failing on the portal's own login screen too. At that moment the portal was in no position to answer “is this session valid”.

It was not our verdict, it was a notice from above#

One thing separates here. Our own periodic check was already treating a communication failure as “unknown” and keeping the session. So that check was not what pushed anyone out.

The actual trigger was the session-expired notice the portal pushed on its stream. The console took it, ended the session, relayed it to the browser, and the screen navigated at once. Read from our code that is correct behaviour: we were told it had expired, so we ended it.

But the side that sent that notice was returning 500 from its own validation API at the same moment. “It has expired” and “I cannot answer right now” went out as the same signal, and on the receiving end the two are indistinguishable.

“Log out when validation fails” evicts only the busiest users#

So the prescription pointed the other way: end the session after N consecutive validation failures. The instinct that denying is the safe side when you cannot confirm is a natural one.

Work out who the device actually catches and it inverts. The thing running the periodic check is the browser. The genuinely risky moments are a user who stepped away, backgrounded the tab or closed the window, and in exactly those moments the check stops or slows down. Closing the window stops it outright; backgrounding the tab lets the browser throttle the timer until the interval stretches. Either way consecutive failures accumulate far more slowly.

The user still looking at the screen keeps the check running, so failures pile up while the portal is shaky. The device ends up selecting the least risky users and evicting them. A health check driven by the client cannot detect absence. It is switched off at the very moment watching matters most.

The way back shares the outage with the way out#

The second reason weighs more. A user who has been logged out will try to log back in, and the login token exchange runs through the same portal.

Log people out while the portal is down and they cannot get back in. A few minutes of wobble that everyone could have ridden out is promoted into a full lockout. And the moment the portal recovers, everyone who was evicted tries to log in at once and obstructs the recovery.

The third reason is the bound that already exists. A session carries an idle timeout and an absolute expiry, so the exposure window is already finite. What fail-closed additionally prevents is a narrow corner, such as a session revoked moments before the outage, and even that falls inside the existing bound. What is gained is narrow; what is lost is everything.

Fail-closed is right where the decision is whether to grant access. It is too much where the decision is whether to keep a session that is already bounded.

Separating what is deterministic from what is best effort#

What needed fixing was not “do they leave” but “do they leave in silence”. So the rule was reduced to one. Every forced exit caused by the upstream portal navigates only after a confirmation. There are no exceptions.

That leaves which line to show: “we are leaving because the link is unstable” or “we are leaving because the session really expired”. That part is a guess. A short-lived trace of recent instability is kept per user and the wording is chosen from it.

This is the core of the design. The cost of a wrong guess is confined to the wording. If the trace is wrong the user still sees a confirmation before leaving; the only thing that goes wrong is one line of explanation inside it. Safety does not rest on the guess, only the wording does.

Classifying the reason once and having all three paths share that classification comes from the same principle. If the 401 header, the stream notice and the periodic check each decide a reason on their own, whichever arrives first fixes the wording, and different people see different lines for one event.

A gate that reads a cookie is always false once the cookie is cleared#

The review found the very defect being fixed living inside the fix.

The gate on the screen decided “is this a portal session” by reading the expiry cookie. But the 401 response that triggers that decision clears the same cookie. By the time the condition is evaluated the cookie is gone, so the test is always false and the exit happens with no warning.

The fix was to decide on the presence of the reason header alone, which makes it independent of whether the cookie survived. The design was not wrong; building a condition on a value that gets cleared was. Put something that disappears into a condition and the condition is missing exactly when it is needed.

What this article does not claim#

  • There are no numbers. Nothing here was measured. The evidence is site logs and a design decision, and the recurrence rate after the fix was not measured either. There is no before and after to compare.
  • It never reproduced in house. The event came out of one site's configuration, so nothing can be said about how often it happens in general.
  • The portal is another team's system. What this covers is the response on the receiving side; why the portal sent that notice is outside my scope.
  • This is not a general claim that fail-closed is bad. What was rejected is applying it again to a session that is already bounded.

Closing#

  • “I cannot confirm” and “it is invalid” are different. Send them as one signal and the receiver cannot separate them; handle them as one case and a wobble becomes a full outage.
  • When you add a safeguard, draw who it lands on first. This one was stepping around the risky side and landing only on the safe one.
  • Separate the layers even inside one feature. Safety does not rest on a guess; wording may.
  • A condition built on a value that gets cleared is missing exactly when it is needed.
